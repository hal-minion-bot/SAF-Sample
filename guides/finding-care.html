Finding & Accessing Care in Canada

Where care comes from, how to qualify, what it might cost, and the first call to make.

Start with the public system

In Canada, most care for seniors starts with the public health system in your province or territory. Home care, in-home nursing, personal support, and access to long-term care are usually arranged through a regional or provincial body — sometimes called Home and Community Care, a health authority, or a local health team. It's almost always worth starting here, because publicly funded services can cover a lot before you ever pay out of pocket.

How an assessment works

To receive most publicly funded services, your loved one needs an assessment. A care coordinator or nurse will ask about daily activities — bathing, dressing, meals, mobility, memory — and may visit the home. The goal is to match the level of support to the actual need. It helps to write down concrete examples beforehand (for instance, "can't manage stairs without help" rather than "not doing well"), because specifics shape the plan you're offered.

What you may pay out of pocket

Some services are fully covered, and some carry a fee based on income or the type of care. Personal support and home-care hours are often subsidized; private companions, extra hours beyond what's approved, and most residential care charge a daily or monthly rate. Ask plainly what is covered, what is income-tested, and what is fully private — and ask whether subsidies or tax credits apply before assuming you'll pay full price.

Who to call first

If you don't know where to begin, call your provincial health line (often 811) and ask to be connected to home and community care for your area. Your family doctor's office can also make a referral. Keep a small notebook of names, dates, and reference numbers — you'll be passed between people, and that record saves hours later.

Your next step

Pick one action today: book the assessment, or make the first phone call to find out who arranges care in your region. Getting into the system is the hardest part — once there's a file and a coordinator, the path forward gets much clearer.
